529 & College Savings

Start early, stay flexible, and let compound growth do the heavy lifting.

What Is a 529 Plan?

A 529 is a tax-advantaged savings account designed specifically for education expenses. Think of it as a Roth IRA for college: your money grows tax-free and comes out tax-free when used for qualified costs.

  • 📈
    Tax-free growth Investment earnings in a 529 are never taxed at the federal level: not while they grow and not when you withdraw them for education. That means more of your money actually goes toward tuition.
  • 🏛️
    State tax deductions Over 30 states offer a state income tax deduction or credit for 529 contributions. In some states, you can deduct up to $10,000 per year. Check your state's plan. The savings add up fast.
  • 📚
    Qualified expenses are broader than you think 529 funds cover tuition, room and board, required textbooks, supplies, computers, internet access, and even up to $10,000/year for K-12 private school tuition. Student loan repayment (up to $10,000 lifetime) also qualifies.
  • 👨‍👩‍👧
    Flexible beneficiary rules You own the account, not your child. The beneficiary can be changed to a sibling, cousin, or even yourself at any time. One child doesn't use it? Transfer it. No penalties, no tax hit.
  • 🚀
    How to open one Go directly to your state's 529 website (not through a financial advisor; direct-sold plans have lower fees). You'll need your Social Security number, the beneficiary's SSN, and a bank account. Most plans let you start with as little as $25.

How Much Should You Save?

The "right" amount depends on when you start, where your child might go to school, and how much financial aid you expect. Here's a practical framework.

  • 👶
    Starting at birth Saving $250/month from birth, you'd have roughly $100,000 by age 18 (assuming 6% annual returns). That covers the average four-year cost at a public in-state university. Even $100/month gets you to $40,000+.
  • 💒
    Starting at age 5 You have 13 years of growth. To reach $80,000, you'd need about $300/month. The window is smaller, so slightly larger contributions help. Lump-sum gifts from grandparents early on make a big difference here.
  • 🔟
    Starting at age 10 With only 8 years, compound growth has less time to work. To reach $60,000, plan on $500/month. But remember: every dollar saved is a dollar not borrowed at 5-7% interest.
  • 📐
    The "thirds" framework A realistic benchmark: plan to cover one-third from savings (529), one-third from current income during college years, and one-third from financial aid, scholarships, or modest loans. This takes the pressure off any single source.

Choosing the Right Plan

Not all 529 plans are created equal. Fees, investment options, and tax benefits vary significantly between plans.

  • 🏷️
    Direct-sold vs. advisor-sold Direct-sold plans (bought from the state website) have significantly lower fees, often 0.10–0.30% annually vs. 0.50–1.00%+ for advisor-sold plans. Over 18 years, that fee difference can cost you $10,000–$20,000 in lost growth.
  • 🗺️
    Your state's plan vs. out-of-state If your state offers a tax deduction, start there. The tax savings are hard to beat. If your state has no income tax or no deduction (like California), shop around. Utah's my529 and Nevada's Vanguard plan are consistently top-rated.
  • ⚙️
    Age-based vs. static portfolios Age-based portfolios automatically shift from aggressive (stocks) to conservative (bonds) as your child gets closer to college. They're ideal if you want a set-it-and-forget-it approach. Static portfolios give you more control but require periodic rebalancing.
  • 💲
    Fee comparison matters Look at the total annual asset-based fee (expense ratio + program management fee). Anything under 0.25% is excellent. Use Savingforcollege.com's comparison tool to stack plans side by side.

Beyond 529: Other Options

A 529 is the default choice for most families, but it's not the only one. Here's how the alternatives compare.

  • 📘
    Coverdell ESA Similar tax benefits to a 529, but with a $2,000/year contribution limit and income restrictions ($110K single / $220K married). Can be used for K-12 and college. Best as a supplement to, not a replacement for, a 529.
  • 🔐
    Custodial accounts (UTMA/UGMA) Money is held in the child's name, and they gain full control at age 18–21 (depending on state). No restrictions on use, but no tax advantages, and assets count heavily against financial aid. Best for families not expecting aid.
  • 🌱
    Roth IRA for education Roth IRA contributions (not earnings) can be withdrawn penalty-free for education. The advantage: if your child doesn't go to college, the money stays in your retirement account. The downside: it reduces your own retirement savings and has contribution limits.
  • 📊
    Taxable brokerage account No contribution limits, no withdrawal restrictions, total flexibility. You'll pay capital gains taxes on growth, but there are no penalties for non-education use. Good for families who want maximum optionality.
💡
Bottom line

A 529 is not the only option, but for most families it's the best starting point. The tax-free growth, high contribution limits, and flexible beneficiary rules make it the workhorse of college savings. Layer in other accounts as needed.

Common 529 Mistakes

Even savvy parents make these errors. Knowing the pitfalls upfront can save you thousands.

  • ⚠️
    Over-funding the account If you save more than your child needs, the excess faces a 10% penalty plus income tax on earnings when withdrawn for non-qualified expenses. Aim to fund about 70-80% of projected costs and plan to cover the rest from income or aid.
  • 👤
    Forgetting to update the beneficiary Life changes. Your child might get a full scholarship, skip college, or you might have another child. Review the beneficiary annually. Changing it is free and takes about 10 minutes online.
  • 🚫
    Using funds for non-qualified expenses Withdrawals for non-qualified expenses get hit with a 10% penalty on earnings plus ordinary income tax. Off-campus apartment deposits, car payments, and fraternity dues don't count. Keep receipts and know the rules.
  • 🐢
    Being too conservative too early If your child is 3 years old, a bond-heavy portfolio makes no sense; you have 15 years of growth ahead. Use an aggressive age-based portfolio early. The market will dip, but historically it recovers well over 10+ year horizons.
  • 🧓
    Grandparent contributions without FAFSA awareness Under older FAFSA rules, grandparent-owned 529 distributions counted as student income and reduced aid eligibility. The simplified FAFSA (effective 2024–25) no longer asks about cash gifts, making grandparent contributions much more aid-friendly. Confirm the current year's rules.

What If They Don't Go to College?

A 529 isn't wasted if plans change. You have more options than you might think.

  • 🔄
    Transfer to another beneficiary Change the beneficiary to a sibling, cousin, niece, nephew, or even yourself, penalty-free. The list of eligible family members is broad. There's no limit on how many times you can change it.
  • 🔧
    Trade schools and apprenticeships qualify 529 funds can be used at any accredited post-secondary institution, including trade schools, vocational programs, and community colleges. Welding school, cosmetology programs, coding bootcamps with accreditation. All eligible.
  • 🏦
    SECURE 2.0 Act: Roll into a Roth IRA Starting in 2024, unused 529 funds can be rolled into the beneficiary's Roth IRA, up to $35,000 lifetime, subject to annual Roth contribution limits. The 529 must have been open for at least 15 years. This is a game-changer for over-savers.
  • ✅
    Penalty-free withdrawal scenarios No penalty on withdrawals if your child receives a scholarship (you can withdraw up to the scholarship amount penalty-free, though you still owe income tax on earnings, but no 10% penalty). Same for attendance at a military academy or the beneficiary's death/disability.
🗝️
Don't let "what if" stop you

The risk of over-saving in a 529 is small and manageable. The risk of under-saving, and watching your child take on $50,000+ in student loan debt, is much larger. Start the account. Adjust later.
