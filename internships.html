A practical path from “where do I even apply?” to a role that actually builds your resume, skills, and network without burning out on busywork.

Why internships matter for building wealth

Wealth isn’t only what you save. It’s also what you’re able to earn, learn, and access over time. Internships are one of the fastest ways students turn potential into proof.

  • Real-market income and options. Paid internships (and strong for-credit roles) show you can create value someone budgets for. They often include pay and benefits that beat random part-time work.
  • Networks that compound. Managers and peers become references, mentors, and sources of inside knowledge on salaries, roles, and who’s actually hiring next season.
  • A better first full-time offer. Many firms recruit interns first; one credible experience on your resume can shift your starting salary. Every raise after that builds on that base.
  • Skills that transfer to higher pay. You pick up tools, communication norms, and judgment that make you productive faster so you’re not starting from zero when you negotiate.

Guide sections

Start with a target, not a spray ▼

Mass-applying to hundreds of listings feels productive; it rarely is. Pick 2 to 3 directions you can explain out loud (role type, industry, or skill you want to prove). That focus lets you tailor faster and sound credible in emails and interviews.

Where strong leads come from ▼

Use more than one channel. The best internships often never hit the big job boards.

  • Career center & campus tools: Handshake, workshops, and resume reviews exist to shorten your learning curve.
  • Company career sites: filter for “intern” or “university” programs; read the posting twice before you write.
  • Warm intros: professors, alumni, student orgs, family friends who work in the field. Ask for advice first; a referral is a bonus, not the opening line.
  • LinkedIn (used deliberately): follow companies you care about, engage thoughtfully, and DM only after you’ve done real research on their work.
Your application kit ▼

Hiring managers skim. Make every line earn its place.

  • Resume: one page unless you truly need two; lead with impact (“increased,” “built,” “led”) and numbers where honest.
  • Cover note or email: short: why them, why you, one proof point. No generic “I am a hard worker” paragraphs.
  • Portfolio or GitHub: if your field cares, link 2 to 3 strong pieces, not everything you’ve ever made.
  • Tracking: a simple spreadsheet: company, date applied, contact, follow-up date. Follow up once, politely, after a reasonable window.
Interviews: show you did the homework ▼

They’re evaluating how you think and communicate, not just your GPA.

  • Know what the team does and one recent project or product detail you can reference.
  • Prepare 4 to 6 STAR stories (Situation, Task, Action, Result) from class, work, or clubs. Even small wins count.
  • Bring 2 to 3 smart questions (“What does success look like at week 6?” beats “What’s the culture like?” alone).
  • Send a brief thank-you within 24 hours: one specific moment from the conversation + appreciation for their time.
On the job: turn 10 weeks into a story ▼
  • Ask your manager how success is measured; align your weekly work to that bar.
  • Document wins as you go (screenshots, metrics, feedback emails) so your resume update takes 20 minutes, not two days.
  • Meet people outside your pod: coffee chats build references and clarity on full-time paths.
  • Before you leave, ask for a LinkedIn recommendation or reference while your impact is fresh.
Pay, credit, and clarity ▼

Understand whether the role is paid, stipend, or for-credit, and how that fits your financial aid or visa situation if applicable. If something feels off (no real training, full-time work for no pay), pause and run it by your career center or a trusted mentor. The goal is experience that respects your time and builds real skill.
