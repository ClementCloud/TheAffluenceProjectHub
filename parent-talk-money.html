Talk Money by Age

Money conversations don't have to be awkward. Here's what to say, and when.

Ages 3–5: Wants vs. Needs

Young children are concrete thinkers. Keep money lessons physical, playful, and woven into everyday moments, especially grocery trips and playtime.

  • 🛒
    Use the grocery store as a classroom Let your child hold the shopping list. Ask, "Do we need milk or do we want cookies?" Walk them through the choice. This one habit, repeated weekly, builds an intuitive understanding of prioritization.
  • 🐷
    Coins and piggy banks Give them real coins to sort, count, and drop into a clear jar so they can watch it grow. Transparent containers work better than opaque piggy banks: kids need to see progress.
  • 💬
    Swap "we can't afford it" for "we choose to spend on…" Language matters. "We can't afford it" signals scarcity and helplessness. "We're choosing to spend on groceries right now" teaches agency and decision-making.
  • ⏳
    Delayed gratification games Try the classic marshmallow test at home: offer one treat now or two treats after dinner. No pressure, no judgment, just practice waiting. This skill correlates with better financial outcomes later in life.

Ages 6–10: Earning & Saving

At this age, kids can start connecting effort with money and begin making real choices about how to use it.

  • 💵
    Choose an allowance structure Fixed weekly allowance teaches budgeting. Chore-based pay teaches the link between work and income. Many families combine both: a small base allowance plus bonus pay for extra tasks. Start with $1 per year of age as a benchmark.
  • 🏺
    The three-jar system Label three jars: Save, Spend, and Give. Every time your child receives money, they split it across all three. The ratio is up to them at first, but guide them toward a balanced habit, like 40/40/20 or 50/30/20.
  • 🏦
    Open a kid's savings account Take your child to the bank (or use a kid-friendly app like Greenlight or GoHenry). Let them hand over their own money and watch the balance grow. Seeing their name on a real account makes saving feel serious and exciting.
  • 🎯
    Set small savings goals Help them pick something they want: a LEGO set, a video game, a pair of shoes. Calculate how many weeks of saving it will take. Track progress on a chart they can color in. When they buy it with their own money, the pride is unforgettable.

Ages 11–14: Smart Spending

Tweens are ready for more autonomy, and more real-world complexity. This is the age to introduce budgeting, interest, and financial tools.

  • 🎒
    Budget a back-to-school allowance Instead of buying everything for them, give a fixed budget and let them allocate it across clothes, supplies, and extras. They'll learn trade-offs fast: name-brand backpack means fewer new outfits.
  • ⚖️
    Needs vs. wants at a deeper level At this age, the conversation shifts: "You need shoes, but do you need $180 shoes?" Teach them to compare prices online, read reviews, and wait for sales. These are lifetime habits forming right now.
  • 📈
    Introduction to compound interest Show them the math. If you save $50/month starting at age 12, you'd have over $100,000 by age 50 at a 7% average return. Use a free compound interest calculator together. Let the numbers do the convincing.
  • 💳
    First debit card: pros and cons A prepaid debit card gives spending freedom with guardrails. Pro: real-world practice with digital money. Con: it can feel less "real" than cash, making overspending easier. Set a weekly reload limit and review transactions together monthly.

Ages 15–18: Real-World Money

High schoolers are on the doorstep of financial independence. The conversations here are about systems, not just habits.

  • 🧾
    First job and taxes When that first paycheck arrives and it's less than expected, explain withholding, FICA, and net vs. gross pay. Walk through their pay stub line by line. It's a perfect, tangible teaching moment.
  • 🚗
    Saving for college or a car Help them set a real savings target with a deadline. If they earn $12/hour and want a $3,000 car, that's 250 hours of work, about 6 months of part-time shifts. Making the math explicit builds respect for money.
  • 📊
    Credit score basics Explain what a credit score is (a financial reputation number from 300–850), what affects it (payment history, utilization, length of history), and why it matters (apartment rentals, car loans, even job applications). Consider adding them as an authorized user on your card to start building history.
  • 🎓
    FAFSA awareness Even if you think your family won't qualify for aid, file the FAFSA. Many scholarships and grants require it. Walk your teen through the process junior year so senior year isn't a scramble. The application opens October 1 every year.
  • 🏠
    Cost of living comparisons Show your teen what rent, utilities, groceries, and transportation actually cost in your city. Use a tool like NerdWallet's cost of living calculator. When they see that a $40,000 salary in San Francisco is very different from $40,000 in Austin, location-based decision-making clicks.
💡
Research insight

The #1 predictor of financial literacy in adults is whether their parents talked about money openly. You don't need to be a financial expert; you just need to be willing to have the conversation.

College Age (18–22)

Your child is legally an adult, but they still need guidance. This is the bridge between parental support and full independence.

  • 📋
    Student loans explained simply Break it down: subsidized loans don't accrue interest while you're in school, unsubsidized ones do. Federal loans have fixed rates and flexible repayment; private loans often don't. Help them calculate what their monthly payment will look like after graduation. The number is often sobering and motivating.
  • 🔒
    Credit card responsibility One card, low limit, paid in full every month. That's the rule. Explain that a $2,000 balance at 22% APR costs $440/year in interest alone, money that buys nothing. Set up autopay for the full balance, not the minimum.
  • 🏗️
    Building credit the right way Use the credit card for one recurring expense (like a streaming subscription) and pay it off automatically. In 6–12 months, they'll have a credit history. Check their score quarterly together using a free service like Credit Karma.
  • 📝
    Budgeting on a tight income College students have limited income and maximum temptation. Help them build a simple budget: fixed costs (rent, food plan, phone), savings (even $25/month), and everything else. Show them free tools like Mint or a simple spreadsheet.
  • 🍜
    The "ramen or investing" conversation Should a college student invest? If they have no high-interest debt and can spare even $10/month, yes. Opening a Roth IRA at 19, even with tiny contributions, gives compound interest decades to work. The habit matters more than the amount.

Conversation Starters That Work

Not sure how to bring it up? Use these prompts as a practical reference card. Pick one that fits your child's age and try it this week.

  • 🧒
    Ages 3–5 "If you could only pick one thing at the store today, what would it be and why?" / "Should we save these coins or spend them?"
  • 👦
    Ages 6–10 "What would you do with $100?" / "How long do you think it would take to save up for that?" / "Would you rather have $5 now or $10 next week?"
  • 🧑
    Ages 11–14 "How much do you think our electric bill is?" / "If you had to plan dinner for the family on a $30 budget, what would you make?" / "What's the difference between something being cheap and being a good value?"
  • 👤
    Ages 15–18 "What does 'investing' mean to you?" / "If you earned $2,000 this summer, how would you split it up?" / "What do you think a one-bedroom apartment costs in our city?"
  • 🎓
    College age "What's one thing you spent money on this month that you wish you hadn't?" / "Do you know what interest rate your student loan is?" / "What would you do if you had an unexpected $500 expense?"
🗝️
The golden rule of money talks

Don't lecture. Ask questions. Kids (and teens) engage more when they're thinking through scenarios instead of listening to a speech. Keep it short, keep it curious, and keep coming back to it.
